# Visual language

The abstraction is a restrained editorial response to relationships within the exposed photograph. It is not a depiction of the scene, a repair of the scan, or a decorative film-burn effect.

## Relationship to the film accident

- Treat the retained photograph and original exposure boundary as immutable material.
- Keep generated work wholly within the genuine retained blank-film region.
- Do not trace, continue, imitate, dramatize, camouflage, tidy, or visually replace the boundary.
- Do not extend objects, scenery, light, texture, or perspective from the photograph into blank film.
- Let the abstraction coexist with the accident without making the accident look manufactured.

## Negative space and economy

Blank film is active compositional material, not an area awaiting coverage. Most of the writable region may remain untouched.

- Use the minimum number of elements needed to communicate the selected relationships.
- Establish hierarchy rather than distributing equal visual weight.
- Allow asymmetry and unresolved space when supported by the photograph.
- Do not fill corners or balance empty areas automatically.
- If removing an element does not weaken the relationship to the source, remove it.

## Source-derived form and color

- Choose forms only after the source relationships have been identified.
- Let scale, placement, repetition, spacing, density, and proportion follow those relationships.
- Use color selectively from visibly important source colors when it strengthens the derivation; monochrome or near-monochrome may be more appropriate.
- Do not import a recurring palette, shape family, text position, grid, or layout across photographs.
- Keep the abstraction distinct from literal illustration and from decorative style imitation.

Consistency across a series should come from the process: preserve the source frame, derive, reduce, and validate. It must not come from a signature motif template.

## Hierarchy

The exposed photograph remains the evidentiary source. The abstraction should have enough presence to express its derivation without overwhelming the retained photograph or turning the original exposure boundary into a secondary effect.

Evaluate the whole composition—including untouched blank film—at the source's original dimensions and aspect ratio by default, or at the different output format explicitly requested by the user. Do not judge the generated elements in isolation.

## Typography

Treat typography as another source-derived compositional element. Its scale, placement, orientation, spacing, alignment, capitalization, and type treatment should respond to the individual photograph, abstraction, and remaining blank-film geometry rather than follow a fixed template.

Keep text wholly inside genuine writable blank film, visually restrained, secondary to the photograph and abstraction, and surrounded by substantial negative space. Do not make typography dominant unless the particular source clearly supports that hierarchy.

## Anti-patterns

Reject results that:

- use generic editorial geometry that could accompany nearly any photograph;
- repeat circles, rectangles, grids, arcs, lines, translucent overlays, or Bauhaus-like forms without source justification;
- decorate most of the writable region or treat blankness as unfinished;
- continue, reconstruct, or illustrate the photographed scene;
- imitate or add flare, leakage, fogging, burn color, exposure gradients, or damaged-film texture;
- derive graphic shapes from the film-burn boundary instead of from the photograph;
- reuse a prior composition with only palette or position changes;
- impose a house palette or fixed typographic arrangement; or
- use unsupported text to manufacture a specific narrative, event, or context.

When a design feels generic, do not polish it. Return to the photograph and derive a more specific grammar.
