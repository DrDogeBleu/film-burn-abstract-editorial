# Source derivation

Derive the artwork from the exposed photograph only after the output geometry and writable blank-film region have been established. Preserve the source's original dimensions and aspect ratio by default; change them only when the user explicitly requests another output format, never merely to create writable space. The writable region constrains placement; it does not decide the visual grammar.

## Inspect this photograph

Describe the photograph in structural relationships rather than as an inventory of objects. Look for approximately 3–5 features that genuinely distinguish this image, such as:

- dominant visual masses or characteristic proportions;
- strong spatial axes or directional relationships;
- repetition, rhythm, grouping, or interruption;
- relative scale and foreground/background organization;
- density opposed to emptiness;
- geometric structure opposed to organic form;
- light/shadow organization; or
- a restrained subset of visibly important source colors.

Do not use every category. Prefer a small set with clear visual evidence. A relationship such as “three compressed forms diminish into depth” is more useful than merely naming the three objects.

## Select discriminating relationships

Retain a candidate only when it:

1. is visibly established by the photograph;
2. contributes materially to its identity or organization;
3. can be translated without depicting or extending the scene; and
4. is more specific than an observation that would fit many unrelated photographs.

Record the visible evidence for each selected relationship. Do not infer unseen events, causes, consequences, memories, or specific emotional narratives. Visual atmosphere, tension, rhythm, or feeling may be interpreted when visibly supported.

## Derive the abstract grammar

For each selected relationship:

1. State the visible evidence.
2. Express the relationship without naming a preferred graphic device.
3. Decide whether it needs representation in the abstraction.
4. Only then choose the minimal form, scale, spacing, hierarchy, color, or material behavior that conveys it.
5. Confirm that the result abstracts the relationship without illustrating the object or continuing the scene.

Use the chain:

> visible evidence → distinctive relationship → abstract principle → minimal graphic expression

Do not begin with circles, rectangles, grids, arcs, lines, translucent overlays, Bauhaus forms, or any other fashionable device. Any device is permissible only after this photograph justifies it.

The original film-burn boundary may affect where artwork can exist, but it is not a motif to echo, extend, trace, balance, or embellish.

## Test source specificity

Ask:

> Could essentially this same abstract composition have been generated from a substantially different photograph?

If yes, identify and discard the generic decisions. Return to the visible evidence and choose more discriminating relationships. Changing only the colors, title, or positions of generic shapes is not a new grammar.

As a final literalness check, prefer translating relationships over preserving recognizable object silhouettes. If an abstract element immediately reads as a simplified icon of a source object, test whether the same relationship can be expressed less literally without losing source specificity. Recognizable form is not prohibited; source specificity remains more important than abstraction for its own sake.

Different photographs should produce substantially different abstract systems. Series consistency comes from the derivation process, preservation rules, and restraint—not repeated motifs.

## Derive poetic text

Derive one convincing, source-grounded editorial phrase, normally 1–5 words. Prefer a fragment to a complete sentence. If the first candidates are generic or unsupported, return to the photograph's visible relationships and derive a more specific phrase.

The phrase may respond to the photograph's atmosphere, relationships, composition, light, space, rhythm, tension, or suggested feeling. It need not describe the scene or function as a title, and should not merely name visible objects.

Before accepting text:

1. Identify the visible evidence or explicit user statement supporting the phrase's feeling or concepts.
2. Reject specific claims about prior or future events, unseen actions, causes, consequences, memories, locations, departures, returns, weather not established by evidence, or other unseen narratives.
3. Prefer language that responds to visual relationships without explaining the photograph or reducing it to an object label.
4. Apply the source-specificity test to language: ask whether essentially the same phrase could plausibly accompany many substantially different photographs. If so, derive language more specifically from this photograph; do not accept a generic phrase.
5. Avoid recurring syntactic formulas, poetic constructions, or phrase templates across a series.

Phrases such as “held apart,” “almost still,” “small distances,” or “quiet weight” may be valid when supported by visible relationships. Phrases such as “after the rain,” “waiting for you,” “the day we left,” “coming home,” or “last summer” require explicit context. Interpret relationships freely; invent facts never.
